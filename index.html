Ways to Manage Social Anxiety Before Networking Events in Chicago

Professional networking can feel intimidating when someone worries about being judged or struggles to begin conversations. Mental Health Therapy in Chicago may help people work through persistent social fears, while preparing a few questions and setting a manageable goal for an event can make participation feel less pressured.

Chicago's professional scene involves frequent networking events — industry meetups, alumni gatherings, company mixers. For people managing social anxiety, these situations can trigger a strong desire to cancel, leave early, or avoid speaking to anyone new. A realistic approach works with that anxiety rather than demanding it disappear before the event begins.

Before the Event

Set One Specific Goal

Rather than aiming to meet everyone in the room, choose a single concrete goal — speaking to two new people, staying for thirty minutes, or following up with one contact. A smaller target is achievable and reduces the pressure of the whole event.

Prepare Conversation Starters

Having two or three questions ready removes the burden of improvising in an already stressful moment. General questions about someone's work or how they found the event are reliable starting points that do not require prior knowledge.

Use a Grounding Routine Beforehand

A brief grounding practice in the minutes before entering — noticing surroundings, slowing the exhale — can reduce peak anxiety before walking in, making the first few minutes less overwhelming.

During the Event

Planning an exit point in advance — a specific time to leave rather than staying until anxiety demands it — can make staying longer feel more manageable. Allowing short breaks, stepping outside briefly, or moving to a quieter area are all reasonable ways to regulate during the event without fully withdrawing.

When social anxiety is persistent enough to cause avoidance, significant distress, or difficulty maintaining professional relationships, therapy can offer more targeted support than preparation strategies alone.

The goal is not to perform confidence but to participate at a level that is manageable. Social situations become more comfortable with practice, and setting achievable expectations at each event is part of that process.

Networking is only one setting where social pressure can arise. Starting a new position brings unfamiliar expectations and relationships, so understanding new job adjustment anxiety can help readers approach the transition with realistic expectations.
